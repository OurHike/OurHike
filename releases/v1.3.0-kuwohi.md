# v1.3.0 — Kuwohi

_48 changes since v1.2.2._

RELEASING.md §5 names this one for the trail's highest point, restored to its
Cherokee name in 2024.

It is the release where the app stops being a set of capable screens a hiker
has to assemble for themselves and becomes one path: first run asks where you
hike, Today leads with what to do next, and planning a walk is three visible
steps — Hike, then Route, then Details — each of them bailable, each of them
showing what it claims. Most of what follows is that rebuild arriving.

## Named beside

**Myron Haliburton Avery (1899–1952)**, president of the Potomac Appalachian Trail
Club from 1927 to 1941 and chairman of the Appalachian Trail Conference from 1931
until his death ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myron_Avery)). Everything
below is from the ATC's own published history — Robert A. Rubin's "The Short,
Brilliant Life of Myron Avery", in
[*Trail Years: A History of the Appalachian Trail Conference*](https://appalachiantrail.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Trail_Years.pdf)
— because a release named after somebody should be checkable against what his own
organization printed.

Rubin opens the profile like this: Avery **"should have died on a mountain, pushing
his measuring wheel along the ridgeline, recording the details of some newly opened
segment of the Appalachian Trail."** He borrowed that wheel from Judge Arthur Perkins
in the spring of 1928, and **"afterward, Avery was rarely photographed without it."**
He walked **"every mile of the Trail, measuring and taking notes as he went"**, and by
1936 **"he had walked and measured every step of the flagged or constructed route and
become the first '2,000-miler' on the footpath."**

The measuring was never the whole job, and that is the part this release is named for.
The same passage has him **"flagging and cutting and constructing and blazing and
measuring"** the routes **"and then writing construction manuals and guidebooks,
publishing them with his own money at first"** — the first guidebooks in 1931. With
Perkins he **"put together publications that standardized the marking and construction
of the Trail"**, and the two-year stretch around PATC's founding saw **"the
establishment of standards for building and marking the A.T."** Measure it, then
publish the measurement in a form somebody else can act on, then hold everyone to it.

Avery's own final report to the Conference says why, and it is the best statement of
this release's goal that we are going to find in somebody else's words:

> Trails should be marked and maintained in a manner to eliminate the necessity of
> labor and uncertainty in finding one's route. They should be an open course, a joy
> for travel. In that manner, without concern for route finding, the traveler will
> derive full benefit from his surroundings. This is what we have sought to accomplish
> in our constant and unending emphasis on the indicated standards of Appalachian
> Trail marking and maintenance.

That is a user-experience principle, written in 1952 about paint on trees: the hiker
should not have to spend attention on the instrument. This is the release where
OurHike takes it seriously as a standard rather than an intention — flow-testing
guidelines, a catalogue, and a ledger that fails when a screen has no answer (#1388);
a room audit that refuses a box as an answer (#1389); a launch budget that says what a
launch may cost and holds it there (#1305). None of those is a thing a hiker can see,
and all of them are instruments that keep taking the measurement after the person who
cared has stopped watching. The codebase got conspicuously bigger this release rather
than smaller, and that is what it bought.

The pace is part of the record too, and not flatteringly. A photograph of trail-blazers
clowning around Avery in the Smokies is captioned **"The court of 'Emperor Myronides I'"**,
and Rubin's next sentence is **"Not everybody laughed."** By the time the Trail was
finished, he and Benton MacKaye **"were no longer on speaking terms."** Rubin's verdict
is still the one worth quoting: **"if MacKaye had the visionary idea, Avery had the
focused understanding that turned the utopian dream into the two-thousand-mile artifact
of rock, soil, wood, and white-paint blazes we follow today… Without Avery, it might
never have been built."** He was also, in the end, **"not able to enjoy it himself… as
a place to slow down, to turn off the clock, to put away the measuring wheel."** A
standard is worth having. Being unable to stop measuring is not the same thing as being
right.

**Two claims this section deliberately does not make.** The white A.T. blaze is a
rectangle **"2 inches wide and 6 inches high"** and **"side trails and shelter trails
use blue blazes"** ([ATC, *Hiking Basics*](https://appalachiantrail.org/explore/plan-and-prepare/hiking-basics/)) —
that is today's standard and it is checkable. That *Avery personally* fixed those
dimensions is not something any source read for this release says: *Trail Years* credits
the standards to publications he produced **with Perkins**, and stops there. And a
figure repeated on several trail-history pages — that he spent "sixteen years" measuring
— does not survive contact with the ATC's own account, which has him borrowing the wheel
in 1928 and finished in 1936. Both were tempting, both were checked, and the weaker true
sentence is what got written. RELEASING.md §6 is the rule; this paragraph is what
following it looks like when the better story turns out to be unsourced.

**Who built this one.** Most of this codebase, this release included, was written by
[Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code) working against the maintainer's review —
the standards named above among it, and these notes. That is recorded here for the same
reason `@unvalidated` is greppable and for the same reason a steward's round-number
estimate may not be rendered in the same voice as a surveyed one: a hiker weighing how
far to trust a water distance is entitled to know where it came from, and provenance
that stops at the last hand to touch the file is provenance nobody has.

## What changed for a hiker

- The app walks you from first run to a finished walk along one path. Planning
  is three visible steps — Hike, Route, Details — and leaving any of them asks
  first rather than dropping what you built.
- First run asks where you hike, so the map opens on your own ground before you
  ever turn location on.
- Today opens with what to do next instead of an empty list, and keeps Find a
  hike and Plan a hike within reach from every state of the screen.
- Where two trails share one treadway, the map draws both — each blaze on its
  own side of one line — instead of one covering the other.
- The Long Path is named and wears its mark at every zoom, not only close in.
- Walks other people wrote up are on the map as routes you can take, from
  NYNJTC's Favorite Hikes.
- A long hike is something you set up, pause, resume, finish and share, rather
  than a loose set of sections.
- Water on the route, and the shelters and campsites it passes, are listed
  before you save a walk — and left out rather than guessed at when the
  download holds none.
- A serious warning read offline now carries the date a moderator confirmed it.
- Finishing a walk is asked, never assumed. If you leave one open, the next
  morning asks once, with both answers and no default.
- The app opens faster: the map engine no longer has to load before the first
  screen can paint.
- The wrong-way alert is gone. It was built in full, never switched on, and
  never tested outdoors — so it was removed rather than shipped on trust.
- Workdays that were never real are off the map, in every environment.

## What is not validated

**The generator's note here was wrong and is worth recording rather than just
correcting.** It reported that no issue carries `needs-field-testing`; its API
call had failed, and `.github/scripts/release_notes.py` falls back to that
sentence when it cannot list them. Two issues carry the label:

- [#105 — Outdoor usability pass — sunlight glare and gloved, one-handed use](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/issues/105)
- [#1100 — A stationary phone still re-renders the shell at fix rate, and the number that would stop it is nobody's measurement yet](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/issues/1100)

Standing, and named in RELEASING.md §8d itself:

- [#91](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/issues/91) — cumulative ascent. The
  check exists and deliberately fails for want of reference figures.
- [#94](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/issues/94) — end-to-end verification
  against real published artifacts.

New with this release, both from the two-tone treatment for two trails on one
treadway:

- **Each blaze on a shared stretch is half a line wide** — about 2.25 px on a
  phone. The maintainer chose that treatment from drawn options seeing the
  figure; nobody has read one outdoors. #105 above is where that would be
  checked.
- **The casing that masks the two original lines is narrower than the pairing
  tolerance above about z16.** `lib/concurrency.py` pairs lines within 10 m,
  and the mask reaches ~3.25 px either side of the shared chord: 1.4 px of
  separation at z14, 2.8 px at z15, 5.5 px at z16, 11.1 px at z17
  (156543.03·cos(41°)/2^z, computed 2026-09-14). So from about z16 a pair
  digitised at the far end of the tolerance shows the partner's own line beside
  the two-tone. The measured median separation is 3.3 m, which stays under the
  reach below z17. Stated with the arithmetic and the alternatives on
  `client/src/map/sharedGround.ts`.

## Compatibility

**Nothing is required of a hiker.** No re-download, no re-install, no signing
in again.

A completed map download survives the update. The hash check that discards
bytes the bucket has replaced applies to a PARTIAL transfer only — a finished
archive is served from storage untouched
(`client/src/lib/archiveDownload.ts`). What does restart, once, is a download
that happens to be in progress at the moment the production data republishes;
that is the interrupted transfer the same file describes, not a lost archive.

This build reads the `2026-09-14` data release, where the previous release read
the bucket's flat keys. That pin is what `pages.yml` checks before it will
deploy the tag.

**This release carries a database migration**, unlike every v1.2.x release
before it, whose `openapi_baselines/retained.json` entries each record "adds no
Alembic revision". `a3f6b81d2e94_synced_hikes` creates `synced_hikes` and
`synced_active_hikes` and drops or renames nothing — expand, not contract
(RELEASING.md §8c) — so the release still serving traffic during the rollout
keeps working against the new schema. Row-level security is enabled on both
tables in the same revision.
