# v1.2.0 — Fontana Dam

_38 changes since v1.1.1._

v1.1.0 drew other organizations' trails. This build makes them answer the same
questions the A.T. already did.

The waypoints DEC and New York State Parks maintain are shipped rather than merely
mapped, their closures and alerts arrive through the same delivery as the ATC's, and
the legend finally has a key for what a closure and a serious warning look like. The
day-hike builder is finished — it routes over the network, prices a leg at the metres
actually walked, and draws the elevation under a walk that is not the A.T. Reporting a
problem became a window that opens over the map instead of a page that takes you off
it, and a press-and-hold puts that window on a specific spot. The terrain download got
a great deal smaller, and gained a Light level for a phone that cannot spare the space.

Fontana Dam is the gateway to the Smokies and the place a northbound hiker stages
before the hardest stretch of the south — shower, resupply, register, weigh the pack.
[RELEASING.md](../RELEASING.md) §5 fixes the landmark order and is the source for that
description; nothing here measured it. It is about the right place for the release
where the app stops being a map that shows other trails and starts being one that
carries the same weight for all of them.

## Named beside

**George Masa** (1885–1933), who measured the Smokies with a bicycle wheel.

Masa was born in Tokyo on 6 April 1885, as the second son of a family named Takahashi, and
was known as Shoji Endo before taking George Masa as his professional name.[^1] He scouted
and marked a significant portion of the Appalachian
Trail through the Smokies,[^1] worked with ATC chairman Myron Avery to chart the route in
western North Carolina, and collaborated with Avery to found the Carolina Appalachian
Trail Club, which later merged with the Carolina Mountain Club.[^2] Horace Kephart was his
close friend and frequent hiking companion; the two are credited with naming Charlies
Bunion.[^2]

**The link to this release is the wheel.** Alongside his camera Masa carried a homemade
odometer he built from an old bicycle,[^1] and he hiked with it "not unlike Avery's"
measuring wheel[^2] — because the maps of that country had distances in them that nobody
had walked. This build is largely the same errand: a tap now snaps to the trail's own
vertices rather than the chord between them, a leg is priced at the metres actually
walked, and a waypoint says how far ahead it is only where that word is earned. He did
that work for clubs whose names are not the one on the trail, which is the other half of
what this release is about.

Masa Knob, 5,685 feet in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, was named for him in
1961,[^1] and he was inducted into the Appalachian Trail Hall of Fame in 2018 in
recognition of his mapping and trail work.[^1][^2]

**One thing the sources disagree on, recorded rather than smoothed.** Wikipedia gives his
death as 21 June 1933 at 48;[^1] the ATC's own account says he died "two years later at
age 54" after Kephart's 1931 death.[^2] Both agree on 1933, and the two ages imply birth
years six years apart, so the year is the part to lean on and the age is the part to
distrust. Nothing here resolves it, and this release is not the place to try.

[^1]: [George Masa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Masa), Wikipedia, read 2026-08-28.
[^2]: [Coming into Focus: George Masa's Legacy](https://appalachiantrail.org/news-stories/coming-into-focus-george-masas-legacy/), Appalachian Trail Conservancy, read 2026-08-28.

## What changed for a hiker

**The map**

- The opening map draws every trail we have mapped, not only the A.T.
- Waypoints maintained by New York State DEC and New York State Parks are shipped now,
  not just their trail lines.
- Closures and serious warnings have their own rows on the legend, so the symbols mean
  something without guessing. A closure is drawn as barrier tape rather than a band that
  rendered black, and an ATC point notice as an open-centre burst.
- The ground network draws in strokes rather than ribbons, so the trail you are on is
  the widest line again.
- A closure keeps its designation across tread shared by two organizations, instead of
  the shared stretch losing it.

**Planning a walk**

- The day-hike builder is finished: it routes over the trail network, prices a leg at the
  metres actually walked, and draws the elevation under a walk that is not the A.T.
- Press and hold anywhere on the map to act on that spot.
- A tap snaps to the trail's own vertices rather than to the straight chord between them.
- A waypoint says how far ahead it is — only where the app can earn that word.
- Open a saved hike straight from Today, with a leg showing its blaze rather than its
  steward's name.
- The middle mode is called **Long hike** now, not thru-hike: a thru-hike is one way of
  living on the trail and not the only one.

**Telling somebody something is wrong**

- Reporting a problem is a window that opens over the map, not a page that takes you off
  it. Escape peels one layer instead of losing the screen behind it.
- A shelter is asked what is wrong with it rather than whether it is "broken", a shelter
  is asked about damage rather than capacity, and a parking lot can be reported at all.
- The Volunteer tab has the panel it was supposed to get.

**Downloads and battery**

- Terrain is tapered by zoom, so the Standard hiking sheet is substantially smaller than
  it was. There is a new **Light** level for a phone that cannot spare the space.
- Download sizes are read from the bucket rather than a hand-kept constant, so the figure
  you weigh against your free space is the size of the object behind it.
- The download is admitted on the map while it runs.
- Three things that drained battery for nothing stopped: the shell no longer rebuilds the
  ribbon for a GPS wobble that never moved your mile, published reports are not re-snapped
  once per fix, and the map is built once per session.

**Alerts**

- Notices and alerts from other organizations arrive through the same delivery as the
  ATC's, with NYNJTC registered as the first.
- The wrong-way alert's button is readable now — it, and every filled orange button, had
  too little contrast.

## Compatibility

**Nothing is required of anyone.** No re-install, no re-download, no signing in again.
This release adds no database migration — there are no new revisions under
`backend/alembic/versions/` since v1.1.1 — so there is no rollout window to sit through
and no schema step behind it.

**One thing is worth doing in the right order, and it is the reason to update before you
next re-download terrain.** The terrain taper (#1087) replaced the bytes behind
`dem.pmtiles`, which is a flat key every deployed build asks for — v1.0.0, v1.1.0 and
v1.1.1 alike. Those clients *throw* on an archive miss where this one upscales the
nearest ancestor tile it holds. So a hiker still on a shipped build who **deletes and
re-downloads** terrain gets blank hillshade offline past 6 miles from the trail at z13,
where their previous download drew relief.

The exposure is narrow and `pipeline/export_dem.py` records it in full: an archive already
on a phone is untouched, a mid-flight download detects the republish by its published hash
and restarts clean rather than splicing, the affected ground is past `MAX_OFF_TRAIL_MILES`
where the app already declines to say where somebody is, and blank terrain is a missing
texture rather than a wrong one — the trail, the waypoints and your position all still
draw. What it costs is a hiker who deletes and re-downloads at a resupply stop, which is a
real thing people do.

**v1.2.0 is what closes that window.** Update first, then re-download.

**The Light level is new and is smaller ground, not the same ground compressed.** It caps
the basemap at z12 and tapers terrain harder, so labels and geometry survive magnification
while hillshade detail does not. It is a real choice to read, not a free saving.
