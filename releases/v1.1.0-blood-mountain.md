# v1.1.0 — Blood Mountain

_87 changes since v1.0.0._

v1.0.0 shipped a navigation core for one trail. This build stops assuming there is
only one trail.

The New York–New Jersey Trail Conference's and New York State Parks' trails are drawn
now, with Mohonk Preserve's paths and carriage roads and New York State DEC land
beside them; water is measured against every trail the app draws rather than only the
A.T.; and the organizations that maintain those trails are named where the credit
belongs instead of the ATC standing in for all of them. Alongside that, planning grew
a day-hike builder that routes over the trail network, every time estimate moved onto
the hiker's own pace, and the app now exists as an iOS and an Android shell as well as
something you add to a home screen.

Blood Mountain is Georgia's high point on the Appalachian Trail and the first real
climb a northbound hiker meets — [RELEASING.md](../RELEASING.md) §5, which fixes the
landmark order and is the source for that description rather than anything measured
here. Springer Mountain was the trailhead. This is the first place the walking gets
hard, which is about right for the release where the app stops being one trail's map
and starts being a network's.

## Named beside

**Ronald S. Rosen**, of Poughkeepsie, New York, who has the finest two-line résumé in trail
maintenance: *moved the Appalachian Trail off the road*, and *dealt with the plutonium*.

He started in the mid-1970s as a maintainer with the New York–New Jersey Trail Conference. In
1981 he founded and chaired the Dutchess Appalachian Trail Management Committee, whose remit
grew until it covered every mile of the A.T. in New York east of the Hudson, and he led the
work of getting the trail there "off the roads in this area to a true trail."[^1]

Then there is Nuclear Lake. In December 1972 a chemical explosion at the United Nuclear
Corporation's research complex north of Pawling put an unmeasured quantity of plutonium dust
through the building and out into the shoreline and woods around it; the cleanup ran to some
$3 million.[^2] Rosen "directed remediation efforts following the cleanup of a radioactive
spill at Nuclear Lake near the path of the Trail."[^1] By 1984, soil and vegetation tests
showed radiation "no higher than normal background levels", and the trail — 1,200 feet from
the water — was declared safe.[^3] The A.T. now walks straight past it and the loop is one of
the better-known day hikes in the Hudson Valley,[^3] which is a considerable second act for a
body of water called Nuclear Lake.

**Why him, for this release, rather than because the story is good.** §6 asks for a genuine
link and refuses an invented one, and there are two here. He is NYNJTC — and this is the
release that finally draws NYNJTC's trails instead of the A.T. alone. And he wrote "one of the
first Local Management Plans which has become a model for other clubs up and down the AT",[^1]
which is very nearly the question the corridor view in this release exists to answer: **who
maintains which stretch**. He wrote the document; the app now draws it.

He was inducted into the Appalachian Trail Hall of Fame in 2024 and is an Honorary Life Member
of the ATC.[^1] The plutonium, for the avoidance of doubt, was not his — he is the one who
cleaned it up.

## What changed for a hiker

### More than one trail

- **NYNJTC's and New York State Parks' trails are on the map**, with Mohonk Preserve's
  trails and carriage roads and New York State DEC land beside them. The A.T. is no
  longer the only line drawn.
- **Trails carry their names**, and a name dims along with the line it belongs to.
- **Trails you did not choose are ghosted rather than dropped**, and the legend says so
  once instead of leaving you to work out what is missing.
- **Water is measured against every trail the app draws**, not only the A.T.
- **New York State Parks' closed areas are drawn on the trails inside them.**
- **The map says where it came from**, naming only the stewards whose data is actually
  in this build.

### Planning

- **A day-hike builder.** Say where you are starting and where you want to reach, and
  it routes you over the trail network instead of making you trace it yourself.
- **The Plan tab splits into two rooms** — the multi-day trip in one, the day hike in
  the other — and a saved day hike keeps its ways on and off.
- **A finished day hike gets its own card** and a row on the home screen.
- **A leg is priced at the metres actually walked**, and the mile column is back on the
  card.

### Time, and how far you will get

- **Every estimate moves with your own pace** rather than a generic one, and each says
  what it was calculated from.
- **Flat pace is settable from 1 to 4 mph**, with a separate control for what descent
  costs you.
- **Trails that arrived in this release carry their climb**, as does anything added
  after them.

### Trusting what is on the screen

- **Your downloaded map is not replaced without asking**, and the app says what changed.
- **ATC's trail updates are read in full** — all ten pages, where it previously stopped
  at a page boundary — and republished hourly.
- **The off-route band stopped announcing a distance that moved on every GPS fix.**
- **Alerts can be taken off the map**, and are back at the next open.
- **A place that has been removed still gets a card saying so**, instead of vanishing.
- **The map opens on shelters, campsites, water and privies** rather than every
  category at once.
- **A report for when the app itself failed you** out on the trail.
- **Water you can actually get to.** A water point now publishes only where a hiker
  could walk to it: within 100 ft of the trail, a side trail, a shelter or a campsite,
  and not down ground steeper than a 15% grade. The published corridor is 30 miles
  wide, so the old rule could put a point on your map thirty miles from the footpath —
  and 73.5% of the corridor's points sit past even five miles, measured in
  `build_osm_water_reach.py`. **You will see fewer water points than in v1.0.0. That
  is the fix and not a loss:** what went is what you could never have reached, and a
  water source you cannot get to is worse than no water source at all, because you
  might have planned around it.

### The app itself

- **iOS and Android shells**, built from the same source as the web app.
- **Today** — a dated journal of the walking you have done, with the map given back its
  whole viewport.
- **The same account on two devices** carries your preferences and your trips, and shows
  you the seam when there is one.
- **You can take your data back, or be rid of us entirely.**

## What is not validated

## Release review

RELEASING.md §9's pass over the combined `v1.0.0..79fcbe08` diff — 312 commits, 1,063 files,
~169k added lines — read as one thing rather than as 87 pull requests. The counts, per §9's
fourth step:

| | |
|---|---|
| Findings | **15** |
| Reproduced by executing the code | 3 |
| Classified into §8b's safety-critical set | **4 as reviewed; 3 after one was withdrawn** |
| Fixed | **3 — all of the set that survived** |
| Deferred | **0** — §9 forbids deferring this set |

**What the review did not do, stated because a count implies a coverage it did not have.**
It was a single pass in one context: no multi-agent fan-out and no independent verify pass,
so twelve of the fifteen findings are read-based rather than reproduced. Coverage was
risk-prioritised — the water, elevation and routing pipeline, the new backend routers,
`client/src/lib` in full, and the download path — while the large UI files and the new
`site/` build got targeted scanning only. **A finding count from a partial read is a floor,
not a total.**

**The safety-critical set was four when this file was first written, and is three.** The
fourth — that the water-reachability cull reached only the OSM source, leaving the
`opentrail_at` source ungated — was wrong, and is withdrawn rather than fixed. The gate is
scoped to OSM deliberately: `export_poi.py`'s docstring says so, and `load_osm_water_reach`
returns a verdict keyed by `osm_id` built from `osm_water.geojson` alone, so a point from
the other source has no verdict to look up. Applying the gate to both sources would have
given every `opentrail_at` point a missing verdict and dropped essentially all of the
app's water. The finding was raised in the review, asserted twice in conversation and
written into a pull request body before it was checked against the code — which is the
failure CLAUDE.md's evidence standard exists to prevent, recorded here rather than quietly
dropped.

The three that stood are fixed rather than deferred, as §9 requires of anything in this
set: a dispute rule that claimed a distinct-days test it never ran, a water-reach
checkpoint that overwrote the file its own drop guard reads as a baseline, and a hash
worker that hung the archive check forever when one request threw. They are described in
[#1072 — Fix the three safety-critical findings the v1.1.0 review actually had](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/pull/1072)
rather than here, because they are repository facts rather than hiker-facing ones.

## Compatibility

**Nothing is required of a hiker.** No re-download, no re-install, no signing in again.

A v1.0.0 install upgrades in place: its downloaded archive, its queued reports and its
saved plan are all read by this build. The single-plan store is migrated into the
multi-trip store on first open, and `client/src/lib/storedShapes.compat.test.ts` covers
that path directly — "migrates the plan already on the phone into a trip". The fixtures
it reads are deliberately untyped, so a shape change cannot be hidden by editing them
until the test passes.

The one backend removal in this release is
[#903 — Delete POST /wrong-way-events, the relay no client could ever call](https://github.com/OurHike/OurHike/pull/903).
That pull request's own finding is that no client could ever have called it; on that
finding no client in the field loses anything by its going, and §8c's "removals and
narrowings fail" rule is not engaged. The finding is #903's rather than this release's —
it was not re-derived here.

The caveat on all of the above is the fixture gap in the section immediately before this
one.
